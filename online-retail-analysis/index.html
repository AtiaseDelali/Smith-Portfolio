Online Retail Analysis
in SQL & Power BI

Every online shop keeps a record of every sale, but those records only become useful when someone asks the right questions. In this project I take the role of a data analyst asked one simple question by a UK online gift retailer: who really pays the bills? The dataset, Online Retail II from the UCI Machine Learning Repository, contains over one million transactions from December 2009 to December 2011. After cleaning the data in SQLite, I removed cancelled orders, postage and fee lines and zero-price items, so that every figure reflects a real sale. I then explored the data with simple, readable SQL, using aggregate functions, grouping, filtering and date functions to answer eleven business questions, and brought the answers together in a Power BI dashboard.

The business sold £19.47 million across 44,714 orders to 5,854 customers in 43 countries, and the first surprise was the average order of £435.53, far too high for people buying gifts for themselves. The clues kept pointing the same way: customers order during office hours, the shop takes almost no orders on Saturdays, and a few overseas accounts behave like distributors. Ireland is the second-largest market with only three customers, and Dutch orders average £2,545, five times the UK average. This “gift shop” is really a wholesale business. Its sales follow the Christmas season, with September to November producing the five best months in the data, and just ten customers account for about 14% of all revenue. One of the most useful findings came from questioning a top-10 list: the third best-selling product turned out to be a single order of 80,995 paper craft kits, cancelled twelve minutes later, a reminder to always check big numbers before trusting them.

Data Exploration in SQL

The analysis was done in SQLite using DBeaver. The first step creates a clean sales table that keeps only real sales, so every question works from the same reliable data. Each of the eleven questions then uses straightforward SQL: SUM and COUNT to measure sales, orders and customers; GROUP BY and ORDER BY to rank countries, products and customers; HAVING to compare countries with enough orders; and strftime to look at sales by year, month, weekday and hour.

The questions build on each other like a story. They start with the size of the business, move on to when and where it sells, then look at who its biggest customers are and what they buy. One query checks the “order that never happened”, and the last measures how much was cancelled. Every query in the script is followed by a comment explaining what it found.

Power BI Dashboard

The dashboard brings the main answers together on one page, so a manager can understand the business in a few seconds. Four cards show total sales, orders, customers and average order value. A line chart shows monthly sales rising every autumn and peaking in November in both years, while bar charts rank the top countries outside the UK and the best-selling products.

The final chart shows orders by day of the week. Thursday is the busiest day and Saturday is almost empty, which points to a business that serves trade customers rather than the public. The mistaken 80,995-unit order is filtered out of the dashboard, so it does not distort the product rankings.

Key Findings

  1. A wholesale business in disguise. Orders average £435.53, arrive in office hours and almost never on Saturdays.
  2. Christmas decides the year. The five best months are all in September, October or November.
  3. A few customers carry the business. Ten customers bring in about 14% of all sales.
  4. Big accounts abroad. Ireland is the second-biggest market with only three customers.
  5. Question the top 10. One “best seller” was a single mistaken order, cancelled after twelve minutes.

Recommendations

  1. Look after the top customers. Give the biggest accounts a named contact and early access to new stock.
  2. Prepare for autumn early. Stock up on best sellers before September, when the busiest months begin.
  3. Grow the overseas accounts. A few large international customers can be worth more than whole countries.
  4. Check unusual orders. Flag any order far larger than a customer’s normal size before it is processed.
