Consumer Complaints & Response SLA
A Business Analysis

When a customer complains to the US regulator, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), the complaint is forwarded to the company, which must respond within 15 days. That deadline works like a service level agreement (SLA): a late response is a regulatory risk, and a complaint that ends with the company giving money back or fixing something points to a process error. In this project I take the role of a business analyst supporting a complaints operations leader who wants to know which products and issues drive complaints and late responses, how companies compare with their peers, and where to focus first. Using a recent extract of 4,991 complaints from the public CFPB database, I defined the stakeholders, KPIs and peer groups, analysed the data in SQLite, and built a four-page Power BI dashboard.

The analysis showed that 98.6% of complaints were answered on time, but 68 were late, and companies had to provide relief in 42.6% of closed complaints. Credit reporting dominated the volume at 92.5% of all complaints, and the biggest single cause was striking: in 36% of all complaints the consumer said the information on their credit report belonged to someone else, which points to a data-matching problem rather than a service problem. Because the CFPB warns that complaint counts reflect company size, I compared peer groups on rates instead of counts. The three credit bureaus answered every complaint on time and the six large banks matched the 98.6% average, but smaller companies, with only 7.7% of complaints, caused 67 of the 68 late responses, mostly in debt collection and student loans. Complaints about threatened legal action were late 14 times out of 26.

Business Analysis in SQL

The analysis was done in SQLite using DBeaver. The first step builds a clean table: it converts the CFPB dates, assigns each company to a peer group, and adds simple 1/0 flags for timely responses, late responses and relief, so every KPI is a straightforward SUM or COUNT.

Nine business questions follow, from a data check and the baseline KPIs to the root cause of the biggest issue, a peer comparison and the companies missing the SLA. Every query has a comment explaining why it matters and what the result was.

Power BI Dashboard

The dashboard has four pages. The executive summary shows the KPIs, top issues and where late responses come from. The root cause page breaks complaints down by product and issue. The peer page compares each company group with the average, and the last page sets out the recommendations and caveats.

Every chart title states its finding, and filters let a manager focus on one company group or product.

The Business Problem

Every complaint forwarded by the regulator starts a 15-day clock. Before analysing the data, I wrote a project brief setting out the stakeholders: the Head of Operations needs to know what to fix first, complaints team leads need to know where the SLA is missed, Compliance needs SLA performance against peers, and product owners need the root causes behind their products’ complaints.

These needs became nine business questions and four KPIs: complaints, timely response rate, late responses and relief rate.

Comparing Fairly

The CFPB says its complaint data is not a statistical sample, and that complaint volume should be read in the context of company size. A large company will always receive more complaints than a small one.

To compare fairly, I grouped companies into credit bureaus, large banks and all other companies, and compared them on rates rather than counts. This is what revealed the real SLA problem: the smallest group by volume caused almost all the late responses.

The extract is a snapshot, with 95% of complaints received between 24 and 29 August 2026, so it does not show a long-term trend.

Key Findings

  1. Credit reporting drives the volume. It accounts for 92.5% of complaints, and “incorrect information on your report” alone for 58%.
  2. The root cause is data matching. In 36% of all complaints the consumer says the information belongs to someone else.
  3. The SLA is mostly met. 98.6% of complaints were answered on time, and credit bureaus were 100% on time.
  4. Late responses come from smaller companies. They caused 67 of the 68 late responses, mostly in debt collection and student loans.
  5. Legal threats are a hot spot. Complaints about threatened legal action were late 14 times out of 26.

Recommendations

  1. Fix the biggest driver at its source. Improve identity matching between lenders and credit bureaus so information is not reported on the wrong person.
  2. Protect the SLA where it fails. Track debt collection and student loan complaints daily.
  3. Create a specialist route. Send complaints about threatened legal action to a specialist team with a 10-day internal target.
  4. Use relief as an early warning. Review monthly the issues where companies most often had to fix something.
  5. Confirm with more data. Re-run the analysis on two to three years of data for a bank peer group.
